DIY if…

  • A 1-car or small 2-car space keeps total DIY cost meaningfully below a pro quote
  • You already own or can borrow a grinder and shop vac
  • You value the savings more than the durability gap

Hire a pro if…

  • A 2-car+ space where tool rental narrows the savings significantly
  • You'd have to buy every tool from scratch for a one-time job
  • You want the cost to include a warranty, not just materials

DIY cost breakdown

A garage epoxy kit alone runs roughly $399-$2,489 depending on size and system — see our kit rankings for current options. That price does NOT include: a diamond grinder + dust shroud rental, a HEPA dust extractor, a moisture meter, a respirator with the correct cartridges, and shed-resistant rollers — the full list, sized to your garage, is on our shopping lists. Budget another $150-$300 for tools/PPE if you don't already own them, plus your own weekend of labor.

Where the "DIY is cheaper" math breaks down

The savings case is strongest for a single 1-car space you'll only ever coat once. It gets weaker fast in a 2-car+ garage, where tool costs stay roughly fixed regardless of size but the pro quote and the kit both scale up — meaning the dollar gap you're "saving" often shrinks in percentage terms, while the failure risk (see our common DIY mistakes) stays just as real.

FAQ

How much does it cost to epoxy a 2-car garage yourself?

Typically $550-$900 all-in including tools/PPE if you don't already own them, versus $3,265-$5,065 for a professional flake epoxy install of the same space.

Does DIY epoxy really save money?

Usually yes upfront, especially for smaller spaces — but factor in tool costs and the real risk of an early failure requiring a costly redo before assuming the full "savings" is realized.

What is not included in a DIY epoxy kit price?

Surface prep tools (grinder, dust extraction), a moisture meter, PPE (respirator + cartridges), and application tools (shed-resistant rollers) — all sold separately.

Is a professional quote negotiable?

Sometimes, especially on timing or add-ons — but the core prep and material costs reflect real labor and product tiers, so an unusually low quote is more often a red flag than a deal.
